US/China treaty gap

**The Ratification Problem**

1. China tends to ratify most of the treaties it signs, with a few exceptions  
2. The US doesn’t. The US has one of the worst ratification records of any major democracy, and the mechanism is structural, and connected to the US political system and political polarisation.   
3. The two-thirds Senate majority required for ratification is a serious bottleneck, with ratification of a significant number of treaties signed after 1990 blocked by Republican senators for various ideological reasons.   
4. The headline unratified treaties are striking. They include CEDAW, the Convention on the Rights of the Child, the Ottawa Mine Ban Treaty, the Convention on Cluster Munitions, the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, and the Optional Protocol to the Convention against Torture ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_treaties_unsigned_or_unratified_by_the_United_States))  
5. The “Convention on the Rights of the Child” is the most embarrassing single example \- the US is the only UN member state that has not ratified it (Somalia ratified in 2015)  
6. Then there is UNCLOS \- Law of the Sea. The US refused to sign UNCLOS in 1982, though it has treated the navigation rules as customary law since 1983. It signed the 1994 agreement revising the seabed provisions, but has ratified neither. This is despite the Pentagon, multiple Secretaries of State, and even George W. Bush urging the Senate to do so. The US then criticises China's South China Sea behaviour using a legal framework it refuses to formally join.  
7. The Rome Statute was signed by Clinton but ["unsigned" by Bush in 2002](https://www.icj.org/us-unsigning-of-icc-treaty-threatens-grave-consequences-for-international-law/) citing risks of politicised prosecutions against American service members deployed abroad.   
8. The Kyoto Protocol was signed but never submitted for ratification. The CTBT was signed in 1996 and has sat unratified since.   
9. A bunch of environmental treaties remain unratified \- e.g . the Basel Convention on hazardous waste exports \- signed in 1990 \- remains unratified.

**The Non-Compliance Picture**

- The US pattern of non-compliance is qualitatively different from China's.  
- China tends to ratify treaties and then quietly not implement them  
- The US often never ratifies in the first place because of Senate Rule & Polarisation  
- The US often complies domestically   
- The US may also act contrary to the treaty's spirit in its foreign policy \- this is somewhat of an inevitability, given the choice to fight wars. 

A few concrete examples:

- The Geneva Conventions: The US has ratified these but the post-2001 period produced Abu Ghraib, Guantánamo, and the CIA's enhanced interrogation programme, all of which generated serious legal findings of non-compliance from international bodies and the US's own Senate Intelligence Committee.  
- The **UN Charter prohibition on the use of force**: The Iraq invasion in 2003 is the clearest case; Syria strikes in 2017 and 2018 and drone strike programmes are also contested. (Libya 2011 was authorised by UNSC Resolution 1973; the criticism is that NATO exceeded the mandate.)   
- The Arms Trade Treaty: As discussed, the US was a principal architect of the treaty and signed it, but never ratified. In 2019 the Trump administration notified the UN that it did not intend to become a party. There is no formal "unsigning" procedure, but the notice ends a signatory's interim obligations and sent a clear signal. China acceded in 2020. 

**The Key Analytical Distinction**

For your piece, the most important point is probably this: the US and China fail at *different stages* of your process model. The US fails most visibly at ratification \- the Senate is a genuine veto point that produces a large signed-but-not-ratified stack. China has almost no ratification bottleneck (the NPC Standing Committee is a rubber stamp) but has a much larger implementation and compliance gap downstream.

There is also a legitimacy dimension worth noting. The US message that China is "violating" UNCLOS simply does not resonate with many nations, which recognise that America has not even ratified the instrument. [Carnegie Endowment for International Peace](https://carnegieendowment.org/posts/2023/05/rule-by-law-chinas-increasingly-global-legal-reach?lang=en) This is a recurring problem for US criticism of Chinese behaviour \- it is often substantively correct but procedurally hypocritical, which China exploits very effectively in multilateral forums.

The most defensible framing for your piece is probably that **the two countries fail differently rather than one being clearly worse**: the US has a structural ratification problem rooted in its constitutional design, combined with a tendency to exempt its own military conduct from frameworks it otherwise champions; China has a ratification-to-implementation gap, combined with a pattern of signing treaties to gain legitimacy whilst reserving the right to interpret obligations very narrowly. Both are forms of selective engagement with international law \- they just look different on a treaty compliance spreadsheet.
